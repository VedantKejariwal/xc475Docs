## Job Story

**Not collected.** Abir was never asked to describe a time he searched for housing, and did not
volunteer one. No job story, no 5 W's, no root cause.

What the recordings contain instead is an extended argument about whether the product can
lawfully and viably exist. Summarized, because it is the actual content:

**The permission and defamation problem.** His central objection: to publish negative
information about a specific private property you need the owner's consent, and no owner will
give it. *"To write anything negative or anything about any apartment, you need that apartment
owner's permission."* He works through the incentive directly — if your dashboard says a unit
needs $50,000 of renovation, the landlord will not list with you. *"Why would you advertise
your apartment on your dashboard?"* He contrasts this with **Forbes**, which writes about
businesses and *"has the set of lawyers"* to absorb the risk: *"You as a starting company can't
do this."* He warns that US real estate law *"reads each word inside."*

**The Zillow comparison.** Zillow, he argues, carries **facts and estimates, not reviews**, and
deliberately so. Estimates are never fixed prices, because a published $5,500 figure would
destroy the landlord's bargaining position — the landlord can always argue renovation or
comparables. Ved pushed back that Zillow's users are the public, not landlords, and that the
dashboard would be public too.

**The government-versus-private distinction — and Ved's counter.** Ved's strongest response was
that the data is already published by the City Council, so he is only re-presenting public
information. Abir accepted this was *"a big loophole"* worth checking but held that *"you are a
private entity, the city council is a government."* Ved pressed with the US national debt
analogy — government figures appear on private sites — and Abir conceded the point was good and
told him to verify it with the professor. **This is unresolved and is the single question most
worth settling.**

**A partial workaround he offered.** Rather than publishing unilaterally, make transparency a
selling point landlords opt into: a *"transparency meter"* where a landlord agrees in the
contract to have both positive and negative attributes shown, and good landlords are rewarded
for it. *"You can only play around with the rules."*

**Competition.** Zillow could replicate this *"in 2 seconds"* with a bigger audience, better
data and more engineers. His question — *"why didn't they do this?"* — is the right one.

**Market size.** His most quantitative objection. Boston's addressable market is *"about
300,000, 400,000"* and *"that is also a stretch"*; BU takes 2,000–3,000 international students a
year, and across nine or ten area universities perhaps 70,000–90,000 annually. *"Your customer
target… compared to the 300 million Americans"* is very small. *"It's a very niche business to
start from."*

**His actual verdict.** Not dismissal. *"The idea is good"*; *"as a starting point, this is not
a stupid idea"*; build from here, but *"think about how to target the broader customer segment"*
and treat the legal exposure as risk to be mitigated: *"You find the risk, you mitigate it."*

Ved's own restatement of the problem, prompted by the pushback, is the clearest articulation of
the hypothesis in any of the three recordings — that a student renting wants to know *"if what
is shown in the listing is actually true or not"* and wants *"one source with all the
information,"* for *"greater confidence before making a decision."*

# INTERVIEW REFLECTIONS

## Was your earlyvangelist hypothesis validated — are they an earlyvangelist?

**No, and the hypothesis was not tested.**

Our earlyvangelist is someone unfamiliar with the Boston rental market who is currently
searching or has recently rented. Abir never described searching for or renting housing. The
checklist cannot be completed from this conversation because the questions that would populate
it were never asked.

This is a **process outcome, not a finding about Abir**. The solution was introduced in the
first three minutes, which set the conversation's frame for the remaining twenty minutes. Once
someone is evaluating your idea, they stop reporting their experience — and the guide's warning
about leading questions exists for exactly this reason.

## Was your job hypothesis validated? Y/N

**No — not tested.** No job story was collected. The only articulation of the job in these
recordings is **Ved's own**, offered defensively at 03:41 of the second recording. A hypothesis
restated by the interviewer is not validation.

## Which of your pain hypotheses were validated?

**None were tested against lived experience.**

Abir did not dispute that the data is scattered — he engaged with the premise throughout and
never argued the problem was imaginary. But **absence of objection is not validation**, and he
had been told what the answer was before he could have reached it himself.

The one adjacent observation: Abir's own reasoning assumes that consolidating scattered public
records is technically straightforward and that the hard parts are legal and commercial. That is
an indirect signal that **aggregation is not the defensible core of this product** — the same
conclusion the Daksh interview reached from the user side, where the real gap was comprehension
rather than access.

## Which of your gain hypotheses were validated?

**None tested.** Gains were stated by Ved, not elicited from Abir.

## What did you learn about alternatives?

Little about renter alternatives; a good deal about the **competitive and legal landscape**.

The useful learning is Abir's question, which we cannot currently answer: **why hasn't Zillow
built this?** Either the data is harder to obtain than we think, the legal exposure is real, the
market is too small to interest them, or nobody has bothered. Each implies a different strategy,
and we have assumed the last without checking.

His **transparency-meter** idea is also the only alternative *business model* anyone proposed
across the three interviews: opt-in landlord participation with contractual consent, turning
disclosure into a competitive advantage for good landlords rather than an attack on bad ones.
It sidesteps the consent problem, at the cost of coverage.

## Key Take-Aways

1. **The legal question is unresolved and is now the project's biggest single risk.** Abir's
   consent objection and Ved's government-data counter both have merit and were left unsettled
   on the recording. If re-publishing City Council data is protected, most of Abir's argument
   dissolves; if it is not, the product as designed may be unbuildable. **This is a question for
   the professor and the City Council contact, and it should be asked before more build work.**
   It is also cheap to answer relative to its consequences.
2. **The market-size objection deserves a real answer, and the other two interviews supply
   one.** Abir is right that BU international students alone are too narrow. Adhiraj's North
   Carolina internship search shows the same problem in any city, for any newcomer without a
   realtor or a network — a far larger segment that does not depend on Boston or on students.
3. **This conversation should be logged as advisory, not as a user interview.** It does not
   validate or invalidate any hypothesis, and counting it toward problem-phase evidence would
   overstate what we know. Its value is in the risk register.

## Biggest Surprise

That the strongest objection was **legal rather than about user value**. We had framed the risk
as "will anyone want this?" Abir barely engaged with that question and went straight to "are you
allowed to publish it?" — a category of risk absent from our hypothesis document entirely.

The second surprise was learning that the professor originated the idea and supplied the
datasets, and that **nobody had yet opened the files**. The `AMI categories` field was
encountered live on the recording, unrecognized. If the City Council is genuinely the client, the
consent question may already have an answer inside those datasets or with the professor — the
single cheapest way to resolve the largest risk.

## Product Changes

1. **Settle the legal question before building further.** Ask the professor and the City Council
   contact directly: may a private entity re-publish and re-present these datasets, including
   derived negative ratings? Everything else is downstream of the answer.
2. **Audit the professor's datasets now** — the bad-landlord pipeline and the Allston file. What
   fields exist, what `AMI categories` means, where the data comes from, how it updates. The team
   is arguing about a product whose data it has not yet inspected.
3. **Widen the target segment beyond BU international students**, as Abir urged and as Adhiraj's
   interview independently supports: newcomers to any city without a realtor or local network.
4. **Evaluate the opt-in transparency-meter model** as a fallback if unilateral publication is
   not defensible. Lower coverage, but it survives the consent objection.
5. **Answer "why hasn't Zillow done this?" explicitly**, and use Ved's Zillow mentor contact to
   do it. If the answer is legal exposure, that confirms Abir. If it is market size, that
   confirms the need to widen the segment.
6. **Reconsider landlord *ratings* specifically.** Ratings are derived judgements and carry far
   more exposure than presenting the underlying public records. Showing permit history and
   letting users draw conclusions may be materially safer than publishing a landlord score —
   and Daksh's interview suggests users want interpretation, so this trade-off needs deliberate
   design rather than default.
7. **Return to the interview protocol.** Run problem-discovery questions before showing the
   concept. Abir would have been a useful *user* interview too — he is plausibly an
   international student renter in Boston — and that evidence was lost by pitching first.
