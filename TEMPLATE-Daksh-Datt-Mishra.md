## Job Story

Daksh described his most recent search, in December 2024, at the end of his sophomore year.
He had lived in a dorm directly opposite his class building in first year, then moved to the
east side of campus in second year, which "kind of made me feel isolated" and was "quite far
away for all my classes." That experience set his goal for the next search: an apartment
roughly **equidistant from the two buildings where he has class** — the College of Arts and
Sciences and the Questrom School of Business. The apartment he signed was not exactly
equidistant, but was "better than Myles."

He searched **with a roommate**, and notes that his roommate, and all his friends searching at
the same time, each had their own priorities — so the search was a negotiation, not a single
person's optimization.

He worked **through a realtor**, who showed him multiple properties alongside a representative
of the management company. He prepared a written list of questions and worked through them at
each viewing. He was initially self-conscious about this — "it may have been annoying for him" —
then concluded "this is literally his job… he did his job quite well honestly." Asked whether
there was information he wanted but could not find, his answer was essentially no, **because
the realtor was an effective interface**: "it was very easy to ask my realtor. He was very open
about things."

The gap surfaced instead **after** signing. He learned that the reason his unit was **$200–300
per month cheaper** than comparable units in the same building was that **it sits next to the
dumpster** — something he discovered rather than was told. The consequence has been occasional
insects entering the room, which he rates as a minor irritation rather than a real problem
("as someone who has been born and brought up in India, I don't think that's the biggest of
all concerns").

The deeper gap emerged when the ABUJA dashboard was described to him at 16:17. On hearing that
building permits distinguish major from minor repairs, he said: **"That is exactly the whole
problem… I did not know that apparently there are two different types of repairs."** He then
named the underlying issue himself, reaching for a concept from class — the **information
asymmetry** "that arises between the investors and the common people who put their money into
the companies" — and applied it to housing: as someone without a civil-engineering background,
he cannot read raw repair records and judge "how severe the damage was… how durable is that
going to be."

A second, unprompted pain runs through his story: he moved off campus telling his parents he
would cook for himself, has "pretty much not done that at all," eats out most of the time, and
is "spending a lot more on that front, which in my opinion is completely avoidable." This is
adjacent to housing rather than caused by it, but it is the cost he volunteers as his real
regret.

## Ranking of Jobs

**Daksh gave an explicit, unprompted ranking of his decision criteria** — the only ranking
obtained across the three interviews:

| Rank | Job / criterion | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — essential** | Ensure personal safety / safe neighborhood | "obviously without any doubt safety comes above anything"; "I cannot risk with my own safety, especially as an international student" |
| **2** | Minimize distance to class buildings | Aimed to be equidistant from CAS and Questrom |
| **3** | Be close to public transit | Commuter rail two minutes away; a T stop behind the house |
| **4** | Be close to groceries, convenience stores and places to eat | Target and Star Market both ~5 minutes away |
| **5 — lowest** | Be close to friends | "this sounds kind of selfish but after all this comes… how far are my friends living from me" |

He re-ranked for a **post-graduation, working** scenario and the order barely moved: safety
first, then proximity to public transit, then neighborhood liveliness and groceries, with
**apartment condition explicitly below those** and dependent on income. Condition matters only
as a floor: "it can't be something that is so damn shitty that you can't even live there."

## Ranking of Pains

Pains identified during the interview (numeric 1–5 ranking was not collected; ordering below
reflects the weight he gave each):

- **Not knowing what to ask or what exists to be checked.** The sharpest pain, and he only
  recognized it when the permit system was explained: "I did not know that apparently there
  are two different types of repairs." **High.**
- **Inability to interpret raw property data as a layman.** Explicitly framed as information
  asymmetry; no civil-engineering background to judge severity or durability of repairs. **High.**
- **Material facts surfacing only after signing.** The dumpster adjacency that explained the
  $200–300 discount, and the resulting insects. **Medium** — he judges the outcome tolerable.
- **Cost of eating out** because the self-catering plan did not survive contact with reality.
  Volunteered unprompted, and the pain he sounds most rueful about. **Medium-high, but outside
  our current problem framing.**
- **Safety uncertainty in an unfamiliar city**, sharpened by being here alone as an
  international student. **High as a driver, though he resolved it through choice of neighborhood.**
- **Quantifying "safe" is itself hard.** He challenged us on this: how would you define it?
  Crime density alone is insufficient — police patrol presence changes the picture. **High as a
  design risk.**

**Pain notably absent:** difficulty *finding* information during the search. His realtor
neutralized it. This matters — see reflections.

## Ranking of Gains

Gains identified during the interview (ranking not explicitly collected):

- Confidence that the neighborhood is genuinely safe, by some defensible measure.
- Knowing, before signing, the things that only surfaced after moving in — such as why a unit
  is priced below its neighbors.
- Being able to understand a property's condition **without specialist knowledge** — hence his
  acceptance of a star rating with the underlying data one click away: "you will only see the
  stars; in case you want to see more data you can click."
- A single view combining transit proximity, groceries, neighborhood liveliness and safety,
  rather than assembling them from separate sources.
- Being able to search a city where he has **no realtor and no network** — the condition under
  which he said the idea becomes genuinely valuable.

## Current Solutions / Alternatives / Competitors

Daksh ranked his alternatives explicitly, and our product does not currently beat the top two:

1. **A realtor — best option.** "Obviously the best situation is to have a realtor, first thing."
   He trusts his, reasons that the broker fee buys good-faith service, and used a prepared
   question list to extract what he needed.
2. **Word of mouth — second best.** "Look for people who are familiar with certain neighborhoods,
   who are familiar with the city." He and Ved agreed they would personally help a friend
   arriving from India.
3. **Reddit** — he calls it "one of the hidden gems" and consults it for public opinion, while
   warning hard against us ingesting it as a data source (below).
4. **Zillow** — referenced as the incumbent; Ved noted, from a mentor who works there, that it
   does not carry this data.

**Where they fall short:** the top two alternatives are *people*, and they evaporate exactly
when you need them most — in a city where you know nobody. Daksh drew this line himself:
if we "move to a random city like Plano, Texas… we don't know anyone there, do we?" **That is
the condition under which he endorsed the product.**

**On Reddit as a data source — a direct warning.** Asked about our plan to ingest Reddit, he
pushed back twice: "I personally think that will be a very bad idea." His evidence was the
**Boston Marathon bombings** (he said 2014; it was 2013), when independent Reddit investigation
produced "a lot of misinformation." Pressed for a fix, he proposed **account-age and karma
thresholds** as credibility proxies, but did not think they resolved the risk.

**Satisfaction with current workflow:** high — but only because he had a realtor. He would use
our dashboard and **would not rely on it exclusively**: "I think I would use that, but I don't
think I would fully try to rely on it."

# INTERVIEW REFLECTIONS

## Was your earlyvangelist hypothesis validated — are they an earlyvangelist?

**Partially — and the partial failure is the useful finding.**

He matches the hypothesized profile almost exactly: international student, unfamiliar with the
Boston market when he arrived, limited prior rental experience, recently signed an off-campus
lease. He has the problem and is aware of it, and he named it more precisely than our own
hypothesis document does.

But he is **not actively looking for a solution**, because he **bought one**: a realtor. Our
hypothesis assumed newcomers are left to assemble scattered information themselves. Daksh
delegated that job to a professional and was satisfied with the result. He is an earlyvangelist
for a **search in a city where he has no realtor and no network** — the North Carolina or Plano
scenario — and much less so for the Boston search he actually completed.

## Was your job hypothesis validated? Y/N

**Yes, with an important correction.**

Our stated job — "understand and evaluate a rental option well enough to make a confident and
informed housing decision before signing a lease" — is real, and he confirmed it. But our
hypothesis implicitly treats **property evaluation** as the job. For Daksh the job was
overwhelmingly **neighborhood and location evaluation**: safety, distance to class, transit,
groceries. Apartment condition — the thing our permit data speaks to — ranked *below* all of
those, and only as a minimum threshold.

There is also a job we had not written down: **knowing what questions to ask in the first place.**
He could not evaluate what he did not know existed.

## Which of your pain hypotheses were validated?

- **Pain 1 — "uncertainty about whether a property is a good fit beyond the listing": VALIDATED,
  but late.** It bit him *after* signing (the dumpster and the price discount), not during the
  search.
- **Pain 2 — "difficulty finding and understanding relevant information across sources":
  SPLIT.** The *finding* half was **not validated** — his realtor handled it. The
  *understanding* half was **strongly validated**, and he articulated it as information
  asymmetry. This is the single most important correction from this interview: **our problem is
  comprehension, not aggregation.**
- **Pain 3 — "difficulty comparing options and deciding in a fast-moving market": NOT
  VALIDATED.** He reported no time pressure and no difficulty comparing. He viewed multiple
  properties at his own pace and felt no uncertainty at the point of decision.

A pain we had not hypothesized and should add: **not knowing what is checkable.**

## Which of your gain hypotheses were validated?

- **"Greater confidence before making a rental decision": PARTIALLY.** He was already confident
   — arguably over-confident, by his own "gullible" admission. The gain is less *feeling*
  confident and more *being correctly* confident.
- **"A clearer and more complete understanding of a property and its surrounding area":
  VALIDATED, weighted toward the surrounding area.** Safety, transit and amenities, more than
  the property itself.
- **"Easier access to relevant and trustworthy information": VALIDATED on trustworthy, not on
  easier.** Access was never his bottleneck; **credibility** was. His Reddit warning and his
  challenge on defining safety are both about whether the data can be trusted, not whether it
  can be reached.

A gain to add: **interpretability** — a rating a non-expert can act on, with the evidence
available underneath. He endorsed exactly this design when it was described.

## What did you learn about alternatives?

The competitor is not Zillow. **The competitor is a realtor, and the realtor is winning.**

Daksh ranked a realtor first and word of mouth second, and both outrank anything we could ship.
Both are human, and both fail in the same specific circumstance: arriving somewhere you know
nobody. He mapped that boundary himself with the Plano, Texas example, and it is the sharpest
strategic finding in this interview — **we should target the moment a renter has neither a
realtor nor a network**, which is not the BU-student-in-Boston case we started from.

He also told us something we were about to get wrong: **do not ingest Reddit** as a data source.
He consults Reddit himself and still considers using it as an input a bad idea, citing the
Boston Marathon bombings misinformation. Account age and karma were the mitigations he could
think of, and he did not find them sufficient.

## Key Take-Aways

1. **The problem is comprehension, not aggregation.** He could get the information — his realtor
   handed it over. What he could not do was interpret it, or know it existed to be asked about.
   "I did not know that apparently there are two different types of repairs" is the quote to
   keep. A dashboard that merely consolidates sources does not solve his problem; one that
   translates records into a judgement a layman can act on does.
2. **Location beats condition, and safety beats everything.** His explicit ranking puts safety,
   class proximity, transit and groceries above apartment condition. Our permit-and-repair data
   — the technical core of the product — addresses his *fourth or fifth* priority. If the
   dashboard leads with repair ratings, it leads with the wrong thing.
3. **Our real competitor is a realtor, and our real user is someone who does not have one.**
   The product's value appeared only when he imagined moving to a city with no broker and no
   friends. In Boston, with both, he would use it but "not fully rely on it."

## Biggest Surprise

That he reported **no uncertainty at all** during the search — the exact uncertainty our core
hypothesis is built on. Asked directly what was most difficult or uncertain, he said he could
not think of anything. Our hypothesis predicts a newcomer struggling to evaluate options; what
we found was a newcomer who had **outsourced the struggle to a professional and was happy with
the trade**.

The uncertainty was real, but it was **invisible to him at the time** and only became legible
afterwards — the dumpster, and the permit categories he had never heard of. That reframes the
product: we are not relieving a felt pain, we are surfacing an **unfelt** one. That is a harder
thing to sell, and it means "would you use this?" is the wrong validation question. He said yes
and it told us little; his ranked priorities and his Plano example told us far more.

A smaller surprise: he pushed back on the demographic/ethnicity data point on principle,
pointing out it "keeps on changing every single year" in a college town, and citing the mix of
people who lived in his own unit before him. That data point looks both unreliable and
reputationally risky.

## Product Changes

1. **Lead with neighborhood and location, not building condition.** Safety, transit proximity,
   grocery and food access, and liveliness are his top four. Repair and permit data should be
   supporting evidence, not the headline.
2. **Solve for "what should I be asking?"** The highest-value feature this interview points to
   is not a map — it is a pre-lease checklist that tells a first-time renter what is checkable
   and what each item means. This directly addresses the pain he could only recognize once it
   was explained to him.
3. **Keep the star rating with drill-down, and make it interpretable.** He endorsed this design
   unprompted. Ratings must be justified by visible data, but the default view must not require
   civil-engineering knowledge.
4. **Define safety defensibly, and show the working.** He specifically challenged crime density
   as a sole metric and raised police presence as a confounder. Whatever composite we build must
   be explainable, or he will not trust it.
5. **Drop Reddit as an ingested data source**, at least for now. Use official
   police-reported crime data instead — which is what Adhiraj independently proposed minutes
   later in the same recording.
6. **Drop the ethnic/demographic composition data point.** Unreliable year to year in a college
   town, weakly relevant to the decision, and a reputational liability.
7. **Re-target toward renters with no realtor and no local network** — relocating interns, new
   graduates and out-of-state arrivals, not only current BU students. This is also the segment
   where Adhiraj validated strongly, and it is a partial answer to Abir's objection that the
   BU-international-student market is too small.
