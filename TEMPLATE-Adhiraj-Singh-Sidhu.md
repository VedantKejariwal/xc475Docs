## Job Story

Adhiraj's story is short but lands precisely on our hypothesis. He needed housing for an
internship **in North Carolina**, in a city where he knew no one.

His constraints were concrete. He had **no plan to move around** — no car, or no intention of
commuting far — so he needed a **gym close by**. He wanted to live in a **community that was
safe**, and his reasoning was specific to the setting: *"because it is a suburban area and I do
not want to risk it."* He contrasted a **local house or a locality without a security guard**
against a managed community with one. Suburban safety, for him, was about the presence or
absence of managed security, not about crime statistics in the abstract.

He was not asked, and did not say, **what he actually used** to run that search — whether
listing sites, the employer, Facebook groups or anything else. That is the main gap in this
interview.

Asked whether the dashboard would have helped, he was unambiguous: *"Yes, I would definitely
use such a platform."* His reason was aggregation against his own criteria — it *"would
consolidate all of these data points into one view that I could actually go through and
filter… very helpful for me, and especially for anyone who is new to your locality."*

He then volunteered the boundary of that value, unprompted and against his own endorsement.
For someone who **has lived in a city for fifteen years**, *"they pretty much know everything
about the city… that particular person would not be able to use all of the features."* Asked
directly whether the target audience should therefore be new people, he agreed — and
immediately qualified it: we should **still** carry amenity filters such as **swimming pool and
gym access**, *"because those are relevant to the people that are staying in the city for a
long time."* He is describing two segments with one overlapping feature set.

He also contributed the most actionable technical suggestion in the whole set of interviews,
in direct response to Daksh's warning about Reddit. Rather than scraping forums, he proposed
using **crime data reported to and held by police departments**, **cited directly** in the
product: the records *"that are actually reported and are inside the police department have to
be genuine, right?"* He noted these carry a **timeline** — you can see when each crime was
reported — so the product could show recency, not just volume, *"so that you do not have to run
into the Reddit comments."*

# INTERVIEW REFLECTIONS

## Was your earlyvangelist hypothesis validated — are they an earlyvangelist?

**Yes — more cleanly than either other interviewee, though on thin evidence.**

Our hypothesis names "newcomers who are currently searching for or have recently rented
off-campus housing." Adhiraj is the only one of the three who searched **without a realtor and
without a local network**, in a market he did not know. He has the problem, is aware of it, and
endorsed the solution without hedging — where Daksh, who had a realtor, explicitly said he
would not rely on it.

The caveats are real. He heard the full solution described before he was asked about his
experience, so his endorsement is solution-aware and partly susceptible to acquiescence. And
two checklist items — has he looked for a solution, would he pay — were never tested. **He is a
strong candidate, not yet a confirmed earlyvangelist.**

## Was your job hypothesis validated? Y/N

**Yes, with the same shift in emphasis Daksh produced.**

The job — evaluate a rental option well enough to decide confidently — holds. But as with
Daksh, the object of evaluation was **the locality**, not the property: safety, security
provision, gym access. Neither interviewee independently raised building condition, repairs or
landlord history.

An addition our hypothesis does not capture: the job is **strongly conditional on being new**.
He stated the expiry date on our own value proposition — after enough years in a city, the job
disappears.

## Which of your pain hypotheses were validated?

- **Pain 1 — "uncertainty about whether a property is a good fit beyond the listing":
  VALIDATED**, in its locality form — suburban safety and whether a community had a security
  guard.
- **Pain 2 — "difficulty finding and understanding information across sources": VALIDATED on
  the finding half** — the opposite of Daksh, whose realtor solved finding but not
  understanding. Together they validate both halves, but **through different users**, which
  matters: no single interviewee had both.
- **Pain 3 — "difficulty comparing options and deciding in an unfamiliar, fast-moving market":
  PARTIALLY VALIDATED.** The unfamiliarity is confirmed; **speed and competition are not** — no
  interviewee reported time pressure. **Our "fast-moving market" assumption has now failed to
  validate twice and should be dropped or retested deliberately.**

## Which of your gain hypotheses were validated?

- **"Greater confidence before making a rental decision": VALIDATED.** The clearest yes we have.
- **"A clearer and more complete understanding of a property and its surrounding area":
  VALIDATED, weighted heavily toward surrounding area** — consistent with Daksh.
- **"Easier access to relevant and trustworthy information": VALIDATED on both counts** — the
  only interviewee to validate the *easier* half, because he had no realtor to do it for him.
  His police-data proposal shows he cares about the *trustworthy* half independently.

## What did you learn about alternatives?

Less than we should have, because the question was not asked — that is the main lesson for
interview discipline.

What emerged is still useful: Adhiraj is the case where **the human alternatives are simply
absent**. Daksh established that a realtor and word of mouth beat any tool; Adhiraj is the
person who had neither. Read together, the two interviews locate our opening precisely: **not
students renting in their own university town, but people arriving somewhere with no
professional and no friends.**

On data sources, he and Daksh converged from opposite directions on the same conclusion within
minutes: **do not use Reddit; use official police-reported crime data, cited, with timelines.**
Two independent users reaching the same recommendation is the strongest signal in this recording.

## Key Take-Aways

1. **He is the segment, and the segment is "no realtor, no network."** Adhiraj had neither,
   and he is the only unhedged yes across three interviews. The North Carolina internship
   search — not the BU term-time lease — is the use case to build for.
2. **The value has an expiry date, and he told us where.** After roughly fifteen years in a
   city, "they pretty much know everything." Our product serves a *transition*, not a permanent
   need — which means retention is structurally limited and acquisition has to target the
   moment of arrival.
3. **Use official police data, cited and time-stamped, not forum sentiment.** He proposed it
   unprompted, directly answering the credibility objection Daksh had just raised. This is the
   most directly actionable technical input from any of the three interviews.

## Biggest Surprise

That he **argued against his own endorsement** — and that he did it by segmenting the market
rather than dismissing the product. Having just said he would definitely use the platform, he
volunteered that a fifteen-year resident would not, and then, rather than leaving it there,
proposed the fix: keep amenity filters like pool and gym access so the product retains value
for long-term residents too.

That is unusually constructive for an interview subject, and it is the kind of reasoning we
were supposed to be doing. It also quietly undercuts one of Abir's objections: Abir argued the
market was too niche; Adhiraj independently proposed how to widen it without abandoning the
core user.

A second surprise: **gym proximity as a hard constraint.** Nowhere in our hypothesis document
does a fitness amenity appear, yet for a renter without a car it ranked immediately behind
safety.

## Product Changes

1. **Make relocating interns and new arrivals the primary target segment**, ahead of BU
   students in Boston. This is where the human alternatives fail, and it directly addresses
   Abir's market-size objection by extending beyond one university town.
2. **Adopt police-reported crime data as the safety source**, cited directly, with reporting
   timelines so recency is visible. Drop the Reddit ingestion plan.
3. **Add amenity filters — gym, pool, and by extension groceries and transit distance.** These
   serve the newcomer immediately and give the product a reason to exist for longer-term
   residents.
4. **Model suburban safety differently from urban safety.** His concern was managed security
   and on-site guards, which crime density does not capture. A single national safety score
   will not serve both settings.
5. **Re-interview him properly.** Two and a half minutes on our best-fit user is not enough. The
   specific gaps: what he actually used in North Carolina, where it fell short, and whether he
   would pay.
